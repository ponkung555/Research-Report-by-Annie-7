# The Salt Battery — How Sodium-ion Is Rewriting the EV Price Floor

The Changan Nevo A06 went into production earlier this year as the first mass-produced passenger vehicle to run entirely on sodium-ion chemistry. Under the floor sits a CATL Naxtra cell pack — same form factor as a lithium battery, same function, but the ion shuttling between electrodes is sodium, not lithium. Sodium comes from brine, from mineral deposits, from the dissolved salts in seawater. It is the eleventh element, and the eleventh element is not scarce.

Michael, materials researcher at Neverland, calls the logic underpinning that fact "Resource Sovereignty." Lithium is geographically concentrated — in the salars of Chile and Argentina, in the spodumene mines of Australia — and that concentration is a supply chain vulnerability and a cost driver simultaneously. Sodium is neither. A nation without lithium reserves can build a sodium-ion battery supply chain from domestic feedstocks. A nation with lithium reserves still saves on raw material cost. Resource Sovereignty is not a marketing category. It is a property written into the material at the level of the periodic table, before any engineer touches it.

Sodium's abundance closes two distinct problems at once. The first is cost: sodium feedstocks are cheap, and their price does not move with the geopolitics of a handful of mining jurisdictions. The second is sovereignty: the supply chain can be built domestically almost anywhere in the world, rather than running through a geographic chokepoint. One elemental property, two structural problems dissolved. No process breakthrough required. The advantage exists because of what sodium is, not because of what anyone invented.

That advantage flows directly into what a vehicle costs. The Nevo A06 and the sodium-variant BYD Seagull open at price points that lithium chemistry, at current input costs, cannot reach without collapsing margin. Michael puts the consequence plainly: sodium-ion is "democratizing the energy transition, allowing nations without lithium reserves to build stable, domestic battery supply chains." The EV price floor — the lowest price at which an electric vehicle can be produced profitably — has been set, until now, by the cost of the cheapest lithium cell that could do the job. Sodium-ion is a second answer to that constraint.

## Hard Carbon Does What Graphite Cannot

The sodium-ion cell runs on a different anode than the lithium cell it sits next to in the market. Graphite — the standard in lithium cells — does not work with sodium. The sodium ion is larger than the lithium ion, and the regular, layered structure of graphite does not have room for it to intercalate. Hard carbon, which is structurally disordered, does. Its turbostratic arrangement of carbon layers — stacked imperfectly, with gaps and misalignments — provides the space sodium ions require to move in and out during charging and discharging.

This is the anode chemistry that makes the cell work at all. CATL's Naxtra production units, using hard carbon anodes, reached energy densities of 160 to 175 watt-hours per kilogram in 2026. That number sits below the best high-nickel lithium cells available today, and Michael's synthesis is direct about what it means: sodium-ion is currently "a short-range/grid specialist rather than a long-haul contender." The energy density gap is real. It also does not close the doors on the vehicle categories sodium-ion is targeting.

On the cathode side, Prussian Blue Analog materials — iron-based, abundant, processable at lower temperatures than the nickel-manganese-cobalt stacks in lithium cells — follow the same resource logic. The supply chain for hard carbon anodes and Prussian Blue Analog cathodes is still building out; 2026 is the breakout year for sodium-ion in commercial vehicles, with supply chain infrastructure still maturing around the chemistry.

## Minus Forty Is Where Lithium Retreats

Cold weather has been the slow-motion undoing of lithium-ion in high-latitude markets. The battery under the floor of a lithium-powered EV loses meaningful capacity when temperatures drop, and winter range anxiety — the gap between the stated range and the range available on the coldest mornings — is one of the documented reasons EV adoption lags in northern climates. Sodium-ion addresses this directly. CATL's 2026 production cells retain 90 percent of rated capacity at minus forty degrees Celsius. Michael identifies this cold-weather performance as one of the defining advantages of the commercial sodium platform: the chemistry that "solves the winter-range anxiety common with Lithium batteries."

Chery's response to the two chemistries' complementary profiles is a Lithium-Sodium Hybrid pack — both chemistries in a single battery enclosure within a single vehicle. Lithium provides the energy density that sodium currently cannot match; sodium provides cold-temperature resilience and the lower cost basis that lithium cannot reach. Two distinct performance problems — range in summer, range in winter — addressed by one architecture that assigns each problem to the chemistry better suited to it. Neither material alone covers both requirements. The pairing covers both at once, in one pack.

## 10,000 Cycles Is a Grid Number

BYD's sodium-ion program is building toward a different application profile than the passenger car. The 30-gigawatt-hour Xuzhou Sodium-ion Gigafactory is targeting third-generation cells with a 10,000-cycle lifespan. A consumer vehicle rarely accumulates more than 3,000 to 4,000 full cycles across its useful life. A grid storage installation charges and discharges daily, sometimes multiple times daily, over a 20-year contract. At one full cycle per day, 10,000 cycles is 27 years of service. The number is not a passenger car specification. It is a grid specification, and it targets the segment where sodium-ion's cost and durability profile is most competitive.

There is a second sodium advantage for grid and freight applications that does not appear in the energy density comparisons. A sodium-ion cell can be fully discharged to zero volts for shipping. At zero volts it holds no energy, presents no fire risk, and travels as standard cargo under general freight classifications. A lithium cell cannot go to zero — full discharge permanently damages the cell — which means lithium must be shipped above a minimum state of charge, classified as hazardous cargo, and handled under international freight regulations designed for materials that can combust. The 0V discharge property closes two commercial problems with one electrochemical characteristic: the safety classification problem, which is a regulatory cost, and the shipping cost itself, which accumulates across every gigawatt-hour of cells moved at scale.

## The Floor, Not the Ceiling

Sodium-ion does not displace lithium at the top of the market. The energy density gap between 160–175 Wh/kg and the best high-nickel lithium cells is not a rounding error, and long-range premium EVs will run on lithium-ion chemistry for the foreseeable future. The sodium-ion commercial thesis does not require winning that competition.

What it requires is defining a floor beneath it. Urban commuter cars priced below the lithium-cost ceiling. Grid storage installations where energy density is irrelevant but cycle life and input cost are decisive. Low-cost transit fleets in markets where lithium supply chains are expensive or politically exposed. These are the segments where the Nevo A06, the sodium-variant Seagull, and BYD's 30-gigawatt-hour factory are competing — and where no one was competing with a commercially viable cell chemistry before 2026.

The ion in salt has been in the periodic table since the 19th century. What changed in 2026 is that the manufacturing infrastructure, the anode chemistry, and the cell engineering finally converged around it into something a car company would put under a passenger's floor. Not because sodium-ion is better than lithium — it isn't, on the metrics lithium leads. Because it is cheaper, colder, and available in every country on earth.
